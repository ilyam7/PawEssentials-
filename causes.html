The Science

Why Sea Levels Are Rising

Two physical processes — both driven by global warming — are responsible for nearly all of the rise we see today.

1. Melting Land-Based Ice

Most of the planet's freshwater is locked away in glaciers and the massive ice sheets that cover Greenland and Antarctica. As global temperatures climb, warmer air and warmer ocean currents melt this ice from above and below. The meltwater flows into rivers and oceans, and because this water came from land (not from the sea itself), it directly adds to the total volume of the ocean.

Greenland alone is now losing roughly 270 billion tons of ice every year, and Antarctica is losing about 150 billion tons. The melt rate has tripled since the 1990s. Mountain glaciers in places like the Andes, the Alps, and the Himalayas are also retreating — many will be gone within a century.

Why melting sea ice is different

Floating sea ice (like the Arctic ice cap) is already displacing the water beneath it. When it melts, sea level barely changes — the same way an ice cube melting in a glass doesn't make the glass overflow. The danger comes from land ice, which adds new water to the ocean for the first time.

2. Thermal Expansion

Water, like most substances, expands when it heats up. The ocean has absorbed more than 90% of the extra heat trapped on Earth by greenhouse gases. As that heat spreads through the upper layers of the ocean, the water becomes less dense and takes up more space.

Thermal expansion is responsible for roughly one-third of the sea level rise observed in recent decades. It is also the slowest cause to reverse: even if we stopped emitting greenhouse gases today, the ocean would continue to warm and expand for centuries.

Density & Temperature

Warmer water = lower density = larger volume. A 1°C rise in average ocean temperature can raise the sea level by several centimeters globally.

Heat Storage

The ocean acts as a giant thermal buffer. The deeper waters take a long time to warm, which means today's emissions are committing future generations to additional rise.

The Root Cause: Global Warming

Both of these processes share a single root cause: the warming of Earth's atmosphere from human-released greenhouse gases — primarily carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels, methane from agriculture and gas leaks, and nitrous oxide from industrial activity. These gases trap heat that would otherwise radiate back to space, and that trapped heat is what melts ice and warms the ocean.

In short: human activity heats the planet → the planet heats the ocean → the ocean rises. Sea level rise is one of the clearest, most measurable consequences of climate change.
