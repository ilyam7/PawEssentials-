Where Land Meets Sea

The Hazards of a Rising Ocean

A few extra inches of sea level may sound small — but at the coastline, those inches translate into miles of new flood risk, faster erosion, and storms that hit harder than ever before.

~

Coastal Flooding

"Sunny day flooding" — tidal flooding that happens with no storm at all — is now a routine event in cities like Miami, Charleston, and Norfolk. As baseline sea level rises, even ordinary high tides reach further inland, swamping streets, basements, and stormwater systems.

High-tide flooding in the U.S. has more than doubled since 2000.

⚠

Stronger Storms & Surge

Warmer oceans give hurricanes more energy and more moisture. When those storms arrive, they push storm surge — a dome of seawater — on top of an already higher ocean. Each foot of sea level rise lets storm surge reach further inland and do more damage.

A 2°C warmer ocean can hold roughly 14% more moisture, fueling heavier rainfall.

⛰

Coastal Erosion

Higher water levels mean waves and tides reach further up beaches and cliffs, washing away sand, soil, and bedrock. Entire beaches in places like the Outer Banks of North Carolina are retreating by several feet a year, taking homes, roads, and lighthouses with them.

Some U.S. coastlines are eroding by more than 6 feet per year.

△

Saltwater Intrusion

As sea level rises, salt water seeps into coastal freshwater aquifers and rivers. This contaminates drinking water supplies, kills crops, and damages forests — the "ghost forests" along the U.S. East Coast are stands of trees killed by salt creeping inland.

Florida, Bangladesh, and the Mekong Delta are already losing farmland to salt.

⇩

Land Loss & Subsidence

In some regions, the land itself is sinking even as the sea rises — a double squeeze. Louisiana loses a football field's worth of land to the Gulf roughly every 100 minutes, and entire island nations like Tuvalu and Kiribati face the prospect of complete submersion.

Louisiana has lost more than 2,000 square miles of land since 1932.

☀

Ecosystem Collapse

Coral reefs, mangroves, salt marshes, and oyster beds — all of which protect coasts from storms — are being drowned, bleached, or eroded faster than they can adapt. As these natural buffers vanish, the coastlines behind them become even more vulnerable.

Half the world's coral reefs have died in the last 30 years.

How These Hazards Compound

Coastal hazards rarely arrive alone. A storm in 2050 will hit a coast that is already higher, more eroded, and less protected by reefs and marshes than the same coast in 1950. That's why scientists warn that the danger from sea level rise grows faster than the rise itself — the impacts multiply on top of each other.
