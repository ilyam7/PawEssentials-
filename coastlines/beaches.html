Sand is on the move

How Beaches Actually Form

A beach isn't a place — it's a process. The sand under your feet is moving down the coast right now, one wave at a time.

Imagine a wave hitting the shore

Here's the thing most people don't realize about waves: they almost never come straight in. The wind blows on an angle, so waves arrive on the beach at a slight slant. When a wave runs up the sand at that angle, it carries sand grains with it — up and to the side. Then gravity takes over and the water (with its grains) slides straight back down toward the sea.

Up at an angle. Down straight. Up at an angle. Down straight. Each grain of sand zigzags down the beach, taking little steps in one direction. Multiply that by trillions of grains and a hundred million waves a year, and you get an invisible river of sand traveling along every shoreline on Earth.

Scientists call this longshore drift (sometimes "littoral drift"). It's one of the most important processes in all of oceanography — and once you know about it, you'll see its handiwork everywhere.

The simplest picture

Wind → angled waves → sand swept up the beach diagonally → sand pulled straight back by gravity → net movement of sand down the coast. That's longshore drift in one sentence.

What longshore drift builds

Longshore drift is the sculptor of the coast. Almost every sandy feature you've ever seen at a beach was shaped by it:

~

Beaches

Sand drops out of the moving water wherever waves slow down — a sheltered bay, a gentle curve. That's where it piles up into the beach you walk on.

↷

Spits

When the coast suddenly turns inland (say, at the mouth of a bay), the sand keeps going straight ahead, building a long curved finger of sand sticking out into the water. Cape Cod has classic spits.

△

Barrier Islands

Long, skinny islands of sand sitting offshore — like the Outer Banks, Fire Island, or the Jersey Shore's barrier beaches. They're built (and reshaped) by drifting sand.

◯

Tombolos & Lagoons

A tombolo is a sand bridge that connects an island to the mainland. A lagoon is the sheltered water behind a spit or barrier island. Both are made by drift.

Where does the sand even come from?

Sand has to be made before it can drift. Most beach sand comes from one of two places:

  • Rivers. Rivers grind up rock as they flow and dump tons of sediment into the sea. That's why a coast downstream of a big river system tends to have lots of sand.
  • Eroding cliffs and headlands. Waves chip away at rocky outcrops over thousands of years. The fragments break down into sand and join the longshore current.
  • Coral and shell debris. In tropical places like the Caribbean, the white sand is literally pulverized coral and shells — biological in origin.

Once sand enters the system, longshore drift is what moves it around. Every beach is part of a bigger network — scientists call them littoral cells — where sand flows in from one source and eventually flows out again, often into a deep underwater canyon where it disappears for good.

Why this matters for everyday people

Once you understand longshore drift, a lot of news starts to make sense:

"Why does my beach keep eroding?"

Because the sand that used to refill it is being trapped somewhere upstream — often by a jetty, harbor wall, or seawall built decades ago.

"Why do they keep dumping sand on the beach?"

That's called beach nourishment. When natural sand supply gets cut off, towns truck or pump in millions of cubic yards just to keep the beach from disappearing.

"Why are barrier islands always shifting?"

Because they're literally piles of moving sand. They roll inland over decades, especially as sea level rises. They're not failing — they're behaving exactly as physics says they should.

"Why do storms reshape the coast so fast?"

Big storms turbocharge the drift — moving in days what would normally take years. Then the regular waves slowly put it back, but never quite the same way.

The takeaway

A beach is not a thing. It's a balance — between sand coming in, sand going out, waves, wind, and gravity all working at once. When that balance is steady, you get a beach. When something tips it (a seawall, a dam upstream, rising seas, a hurricane), the beach moves, shrinks, or disappears. Understanding longshore drift is the first step to understanding why coastlines look the way they do — and why they're changing.
