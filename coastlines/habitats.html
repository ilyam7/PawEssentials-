Coastal Environments & Habitats

The coast isn't one ecosystem — it's a whole stack of them, each one tuned to a particular spot on the line where land meets sea.

The coast is a layer cake

Walking from inland out to the surf, you cross through several different worlds in just a few hundred feet. Each "zone" gets a different mix of sun, salt, sand, and waves — and a different set of plants and animals has figured out how to thrive there. Here are the big ones.

Sandy beaches & dunes

The first place most people meet the ocean. Beaches look bare, but there's a whole community living in the sand — ghost crabs, sand fleas, plovers, terns, and (where humans haven't built too close) nesting sea turtles. Just behind the beach, dunes are the coast's natural shock absorbers: hills of sand held together by tough grasses with deep roots. When a storm hits, dunes are often the difference between a flooded street and a soaked beach.

Why dune grass is a hero

That scratchy beach grass you're not supposed to walk on? Its roots can be ten feet deep, locking down billions of grains of sand. Lose the grass, and the dune blows away in one bad winter.

Salt marshes

On calmer coasts — behind barrier islands, in the back of bays — the water gets shallow and quiet. That's where salt marshes form: huge meadows of salt-tolerant grasses sitting in a few inches of tidal water.

Marshes might be the hardest-working ecosystem on the coast. They:

  • Soak up storm surge like a giant sponge.
  • Filter pollution and excess nutrients out of runoff before it reaches the open ocean.
  • Feed an enormous food web — juvenile fish, blue crabs, oysters, herons, egrets.
  • Store huge amounts of carbon in their muddy soil ("blue carbon").

Roughly half of the East Coast's salt marshes have been lost to development. Protecting what's left is a big deal.

Mangrove forests

Head somewhere warm — Florida, the Caribbean, Southeast Asia — and salt marshes are replaced by something even wilder: mangroves. These are trees that have evolved to live with their roots underwater in salty mud. Their tangled, stilt-like roots create an underwater jungle that's a nursery for countless fish, a perch for birds, and a home for everything from oysters to crocodiles.

Mangroves are one of the most powerful natural shields against hurricanes. A wide belt of mangroves can knock down a wall of storm surge by half. They're so good at it that some communities are now replanting mangroves instead of building seawalls.

Estuaries: where rivers meet the sea

Wherever a river empties into the ocean, you get an estuary — a brackish (half-salty) zone where freshwater and seawater mix. Think Chesapeake Bay, San Francisco Bay, the Hudson River. Estuaries are unbelievably productive because rivers bring nutrients from the land and the ocean brings food from the deep.

Most of the seafood you've ever eaten spent the first part of its life in an estuary. Crabs, oysters, striped bass, flounder — estuaries are basically the daycare of the ocean.

Coral reefs

In tropical seas, the headline habitat is the coral reef. Reefs are built, grain by grain, by tiny animals (corals) that pull calcium out of seawater to make stone homes for themselves. Over centuries, those homes pile up into the reefs we recognize today.

Reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but host about 25% of all marine life. They also break up incoming waves before those waves can hit a coast — like a natural offshore breakwater. Sadly, warming oceans are bleaching and killing reefs at a frightening rate; we'll come back to that on the Rising Seas page.

Rocky shores & tide pools

Where the coast is made of stone instead of sand — think Maine, Oregon, much of California — the action shifts to rocky intertidal zones. Twice a day, the tide rises and falls, exposing creatures to air and then flooding them again. Anything that lives there has to handle wild swings in temperature, salinity, and water cover.

Look closely at a tide pool and you'll find barnacles, mussels, sea stars, anemones, hermit crabs, snails, and tiny fish — an incredibly diverse community packed into a few square feet. Tide pools are also one of the easiest places for non-scientists to see ocean ecology up close.

Seagrass meadows & kelp forests

Just offshore, hidden under the waves, are two more big habitats:

Seagrass meadows

Underwater fields of flowering plants in shallow, sunny water. Manatees and sea turtles graze them. They store carbon and clear the water.

Kelp forests

Towering "trees" of seaweed in cooler waters (California, the Pacific Northwest). They host sea otters, fish, urchins, and seals — a true underwater forest.

One big idea

Every coastal habitat on this page does double duty: it supports an incredible amount of life, and it protects the land behind it. Healthy reefs, mangroves, marshes, dunes, and seagrass meadows are how a coast survives a storm. When we lose them, the coast loses its armor — and that armor is exactly what we need most as the seas rise.
