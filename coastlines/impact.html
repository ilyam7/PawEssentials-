The human side

Hurricane Sandy & Us

Statistics are easy to look past. People are not. Here's what it actually looks like when the ocean comes inland — and why this is personal for me.

The numbers, briefly

About 40% of the world's population lives within 60 miles of a coast. Roughly 900 million people live in low-lying coastal zones — areas already exposed to flooding and storm surge. Every centimeter of sea level rise pushes those numbers higher.

  • 200+ million people could be displaced by sea level rise by 2100.
  • Coastal cities face over $1 trillion in flood damages per year by mid-century without major adaptation.
  • Small island nations like Tuvalu, Kiribati, and the Marshall Islands could become uninhabitable within decades.
  • Bangladesh alone could lose 17% of its land, displacing tens of millions.

A storm I won't forget

Hurricane Sandy — October 2012

Sandy hit the U.S. East Coast in late October 2012. By the time it crashed into New Jersey and New York, it had merged with another weather system to become one of the largest Atlantic hurricanes ever measured — over 1,000 miles wide. The storm shoved a dome of seawater — "storm surge" — into bays, harbors, neighborhoods, and subway tunnels. Lower Manhattan went dark. Whole stretches of the Jersey Shore were unrecognizable.

Sandy wasn't just a storm — it was a preview. Studies after the fact showed that the 14-foot storm surge that flooded lower Manhattan was made measurably worse by sea level rise that had already happened. The same hurricane in 1900 would have flooded tens of thousands fewer homes. In other words, climate change didn't create Sandy — but it made Sandy more destructive.

This is personal for me. Sandy is the storm I grew up hearing about and seeing the aftermath of. People I knew lost homes, savings, and family members. Years later, some neighborhoods still hadn't fully recovered — houses sitting empty, boardwalks half-rebuilt, insurance battles dragging on.

That's the thing the news doesn't always show: a storm doesn't end when the rain stops. The recovery is a decade long, and for some families it's permanent. When I read about Miami flooding on a sunny day, or about families in Louisiana losing land to the Gulf, I think about Sandy. It's the same story — a different coast.

Other places, same story

Miami, Florida

One of the most exposed major cities on Earth. Streets flood on sunny days. Salt is creeping into the drinking water aquifer.

Jakarta, Indonesia

Sinking so fast that Indonesia is moving its capital. Parts of north Jakarta drop up to 10 inches each year.

Venice, Italy

The MOSE flood barrier was built for emergencies. It's now in regular use just to keep St. Mark's Square dry.

Tuvalu & Kiribati

Pacific island nations with highest points only a few meters above sea level. Their citizens are some of the world's first true climate refugees.

The Outer Banks, NC

Houses literally falling into the surf as the barrier islands shift inland. Some homeowners are paid to abandon them.

Coastal Louisiana

The Isle de Jean Charles community became one of the first U.S. communities to be relocated because of climate-driven land loss.

Why this matters to all of us

You don't have to live on a beach to be affected. Coastal cities are economic engines. Coastal infrastructure carries goods, fuel, and food for the whole country. The seafood in your grocery store, the tourism that funds small towns, the wetlands that filter our drinking water — all of it depends on coasts that work. When the coast is in trouble, everyone is.
