There's a lot we can still do

What We Can Do About It

The seas are going to keep rising for a while no matter what. But how high they go — and how prepared we are — is still very much in our hands.

Two parallel jobs

Coastal scientists and policymakers talk about climate response in two halves:

Mitigation

Reducing the cause — cutting greenhouse gas emissions so the planet warms less and the seas don't rise as far.

Adaptation

Managing the effects — preparing coasts and communities for the change that's already locked in.

We need both. Mitigation alone is too late; adaptation alone is too expensive. Together, they're how we get through this.

Mitigation: cut the cause

Switch to clean energy

Solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, and nuclear. The energy transition is the single biggest lever for slowing climate change.

Electrify transportation

Cars, buses, and trains running on clean electricity. Walkable, bike-friendly cities matter just as much.

Protect & restore nature

Forests, mangroves, and coastal wetlands soak up carbon dioxide and shield the coast at the same time.

Cut methane & industry

Plug leaking gas pipelines. Modernize agriculture. Move heavy industry (cement, steel) toward low-carbon processes.

Adaptation: live with what's coming

Living shorelines

Marshes, oyster reefs, and mangroves — nature's seawalls. Often cheaper and more durable than concrete.

Smarter building codes

Elevated homes, permeable pavement, stormwater systems sized for the climate of 2080, not 1980.

Targeted hard defenses

Sea walls, surge barriers, and elevated infrastructure where it makes sense — especially around critical hospitals, transit, and power plants.

Managed retreat

Where defending isn't realistic, the most humane option is helping communities move to safer ground — before disaster forces it.

What you (yes, you) can do

You don't have to be a scientist or a senator. Here are real things any person, family, or friend group can do.

  • Vote and advocate. Climate policy decides emissions. Local zoning decides what gets built where. Both ballots matter.
  • Reduce your own footprint. Drive less, fly less, eat less red meat, use efficient appliances, weatherize your home. Small steps add up across millions of people.
  • Support coastal restoration. Volunteer with local groups that plant marsh grass, restore dunes, clean beaches, or build oyster reefs. Hands-on work, real impact.
  • Talk about it. Climate denial thrives on silence. Just bringing it up — calmly, with people you love — helps move the needle.
  • Push your community. Ask your school, workplace, or town to adopt climate and resilience plans. Most local officials will listen if anyone shows up.
  • Be smart about where you live and build. If you have a choice, factor flood maps and sea level rise projections into long-term decisions.
  • Donate (if you can). Groups doing great work include the Coastal Conservation Association, The Nature Conservancy's Coastal Resilience program, local chapters of Surfrider Foundation, and many regional land trusts.

One last thought

Sea level rise and coastal hazards are scary topics. But fear is not the most useful response to them — attention is. Pay attention to your local coast. Pay attention to the people on the front lines. Pay attention to the elections and the policies and the small daily choices that, together, decide what the next century looks like.

Humans caused this. That's the hard part. But it also means we can change it — and the coast is more resilient than people give it credit for, if we just give it the room and time it needs. The tide is rising. What we do about it is still up to us.
