The big one

Rising Seas & Coastal Hazards

A few extra inches of ocean might sound harmless. At the coast, those inches turn into flooded streets, lost beaches, and stronger storms. Here's why — and how it works.

Two reasons the sea is rising

The ocean is getting taller for two reasons, both of them caused by climate change. They're surprisingly simple once you see them.

1. Land ice is melting and pouring into the ocean.

On Greenland, in Antarctica, and high up in mountain ranges around the world, there's an enormous amount of frozen freshwater — glaciers and ice sheets that have been stable for thousands of years. As the planet warms, that ice is melting faster than it can be replaced by snow. The meltwater runs off the land and into the sea, which adds new water to the ocean and makes it taller.

Greenland alone is now losing roughly 270 billion tons of ice every year. Antarctica is losing about 150 billion tons. The melt rate has tripled since the 1990s.

"Wait, doesn't melting Arctic ice raise sea levels?"

Not really — the Arctic ice cap is floating sea ice. It's already displacing the water around it, so melting it doesn't change the level (same way an ice cube melting in your glass doesn't make it overflow). The dangerous melt is on land: Greenland, Antarctica, and mountain glaciers. That's water arriving in the ocean for the first time.

2. The water already in the ocean is expanding.

Water gets bigger when it warms up. Not by a lot — but the ocean is huge. The ocean has soaked up more than 90% of the extra heat humans have trapped on Earth. As that heat works its way through the upper layers of the sea, the water expands and takes up more space. This is called thermal expansion, and it's responsible for about a third of the rise we've seen so far.

Thermal expansion is also the slow one. Even if we stopped emitting greenhouse gases tomorrow, the deep ocean would keep warming and expanding for centuries.

So what does that mean at the coast?

Higher water doesn't just sit there. It changes how every storm, tide, and wave behaves. Here are the hazards that are already getting worse.

~

Sunny-day flooding

Cities like Miami, Norfolk, and Charleston now flood on regular high tides — no storm needed. Streets just fill up because the ocean is already higher than the storm drains.

U.S. high-tide flooding has more than doubled since 2000.

⚠

Bigger storm surge

When a hurricane pushes a wall of water onshore, it pushes it on top of an already higher ocean. Each foot of sea level rise sends storm surge further inland, into neighborhoods that used to be safe.

A 2°C warmer atmosphere holds about 14% more moisture, fueling heavier rain too.

⛰

Coastal erosion

Higher water means waves chew further up the beach and cliffs. Whole coastlines are retreating — the Outer Banks of North Carolina lose several feet of land a year, and some cliffs in California collapse overnight.

Some U.S. coasts are eroding by more than 6 feet per year.

△

Salt creeping inland

As sea level rises, salt water seeps into freshwater wells, rivers, and farm fields. That kills crops, damages forests (creating "ghost forests"), and threatens drinking water for millions of people.

Florida, Bangladesh, and the Mekong Delta are already losing farmland to salt.

⇩

Land actually disappearing

Some coasts are sinking and the sea is rising — a double squeeze. Louisiana loses roughly a football field of land to the Gulf every 100 minutes. Pacific island nations like Tuvalu may become uninhabitable in our lifetimes.

Louisiana has lost more than 2,000 square miles since 1932.

☀

Lost natural defenses

Coral reefs, mangroves, and salt marshes used to protect coasts from storms. Warming, acidifying water is killing reefs; sea level rise is drowning marshes. As these defenses fail, the coast behind them gets even more exposed.

About half the world's coral reefs have died in the last 30 years.

The hazards multiply

Coastal hazards almost never show up alone. A storm in 2050 will hit a coast that is already higher, more eroded, and less protected by reefs and marshes than the same coast in 1950. That's why the danger from sea level rise grows faster than the rise itself — the impacts pile on top of each other.

How this connects to oceanography

Sea level rise is the headline, but underneath it is pure oceanography — the physics and chemistry of seawater. How heat moves through the ocean, how density changes with temperature, how currents distribute warmth around the globe. Those forces decide how fast the sea rises, where it rises fastest (it's not even everywhere), and how the coastline responds. The street outside your house is connected, by water and physics, to the deep Pacific.
